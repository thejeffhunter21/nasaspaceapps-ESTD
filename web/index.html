How we tested it

The seasonal Mann-Kendall test

Phoenix nights swing by about 25 °C between winter and summer. A plain trend test sees that yearly wave and can miss a slow change hiding underneath it.

The seasonal Mann-Kendall test never compares a July with a January. It compares each calendar month only with the same month in other years, July 2003 against July 2004, 2005 and so on, and counts how often later years are warmer. Then it adds up the evidence from all twelve months.

The result is a direction (warming or cooling), a p-value (how likely a pattern this strong would be by chance alone; below 0.05 counts as significant) and Sen's slope, the typical change per month.

What this can and can't tell you

Surface, not air

Satellites measure how warm the ground and rooftops are. That is not the air temperature a weather station or a person feels.

Nighttime only

These are nighttime passes. Hot nights matter because bodies need them to cool down, but daytime heat is a different measurement.

A trend, not a cause

The test shows that warming happened, not why. City growth and climate change are possible reasons that scientists study; we prove neither.

Not a forecast

Slopes describe 2003–2024. They do not predict next month or any future year.

5 km cells

Each column on the map is a roughly 5 km square, so it shows neighbourhood-scale patterns, not individual streets.
